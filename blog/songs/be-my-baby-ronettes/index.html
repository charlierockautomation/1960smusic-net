Be My Baby: How the Ronettes Cut Their 1963 Hit

By Charlie Gillingham · Published November 24, 2026 · Updated November 24, 2026

Be My Baby is the 1963 Ronettes single that best shows what Phil Spector's Wall of Sound could do.
Philles Records released it in August 1963, and it reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100.
Only one Ronette sings on it, and a dropped drumstick may explain its famous opening.

What Is Be My Baby?

A Song Written for Ronnie

Be My Baby is a song by Jeff Barry, Ellie Greenwich and Phil Spector, recorded by the Ronettes in 1963.
The three wrote it at Spector's office in Los Angeles, Wikipedia says.
It was the group's debut on Philles and became their biggest hit.
The B-side was an instrumental, "Tedesco and Pitman," named for two guitarists on the session.

Why Spector Held Back the First Song

Spector first cut "Why Don't They Let Us Fall in Love" with the trio, then shelved it.
By then he was fixated on Ronnie, and Darlene Love said the new song was his way of declaring his love.
He married her in 1968.
Our Ronettes biography tells the rest of that story.

How Was Be My Baby Recorded?

A Packed Studio at Gold Star

Spector produced the track on July 29, 1963, at Gold Star Studios in Hollywood.
His house band, later called the Wrecking Crew, played the backing track.
It was the first time he had recorded with a full orchestra there.
Jack Nitzsche arranged the music, and Larry Levine engineered the session.
Studio A measured only 19 by 24 feet, Sound on Sound reports, so the players were packed in tight.

Nobody was credited at the time, so this list from Wikipedia is likely incomplete:

  • Drums: Hal Blaine
  • Guitars: Tommy Tedesco and Bill Pitman
  • Keyboards: Don Randi, Leon Russell, Al De Lory and Mike Spencer
  • Saxophones: Steve Douglas and Jay Migliori

The Drum Intro

The record opens with two bars of drums wrapped in heavy reverb.
Blaine said he was supposed to play the snare on the second beat as well as the fourth.
Instead he dropped a stick and left the mistake in, as MusicRadar recounts.
Biographer Mick Brown says Nitzsche suggested the pattern, so the credit stays in dispute.

How Many Takes?

The band rehearsed for four hours before the tape rolled.
Keyboardist Mike Spencer was so punchy by the 40th or 41st take that he played right through the pause near the end.
Blaine remembered only 28 or 29 takes, so the count depends on who tells it.

Ronnie and the Backing Singers

Ronnie was the only Ronette on the session.
She was shy, so she rehearsed in the studio ladies' room because she loved the sound there.
The backing singers included Darlene Love, Sonny Bono and a young Cher, who said Spector told her he just needed noise.

How High Did It Chart?

The single reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 by the end of the summer.
London Records issued it in the UK that October, and it peaked at No. 4.

Why Does It Still Matter?

Brian Wilson's Obsession

Brian Wilson first heard the song on his car radio and pulled over to study the chorus.
He decided it was the greatest record he had ever heard.
He wrote the Beach Boys' 1964 hit "Don't Worry Baby" as an answer song.
Wilson told The New York Times in 2013 that he had played the record at least a thousand times.
Read more about his band in our Beach Boys biography.

A Drum Beat Everyone Borrowed

Producers have copied Blaine's opening for decades.
Two examples:

  • "Just Like Honey" by The Jesus and Mary Chain
  • "B Boy Baby" by Mutya Buena featuring Amy Winehouse

Films and Covers

Scorsese opened Mean Streets in 1973 with the original recording.
It also plays over the opening of Dirty Dancing in 1987.
Andy Kim took a cover to No. 17 on the Hot 100 in 1970.

FAQ

Who wrote the song?

Jeff Barry, Ellie Greenwich and Phil Spector wrote it.
Spector also produced the record.

Who played the drums?

Hal Blaine of the Wrecking Crew played the drums.
He said the famous opening came from a dropped stick.

Are all three Ronettes on the record?

No.
Ronnie Spector, then Veronica Bennett, is the only Ronette heard on it.

How high did it chart?

It reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 in 1963.
In the UK it peaked at No. 4.
