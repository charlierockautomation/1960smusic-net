Together Again: How a Buck Owens B-Side Took Over the Country Chart

By Charlie Gillingham · Published November 19, 2026 · Updated November 19, 2026

Together Again is the Buck Owens song that reached No. 1 in 1964 as the B-side of "My Heart Skips a Beat."
Owens recorded it at the Capitol Records Tower in Hollywood on January 28, 1964.
Tom Brumley's steel guitar solo made it a country landmark, and Ray Charles and Emmylou Harris later carried it back up the charts.

Where Did Together Again Come From?

A Title Borrowed From a Western Swing Album

Bob Wills and His Texas Playboys lost their lead singer Tommy Duncan in 1948.
In 1959 Duncan rejoined the band, and they recorded an album for Liberty Records called Together Again.
Buck Owens noticed the title and thought it would make a good song.
Several years later he sat down at a piano and wrote it, American Songwriter reports.

A Happy Lyric on a Sad Melody

The words describe tears that have stopped falling and lonely nights that are over.
Owens wrote in his autobiography that it is "actually a happy song."
The mournful melody, he said, leads many listeners to hear it as sad.

How Buck Owens Recorded It

The January 1964 Session

Owens went into the Capitol Records Tower in Hollywood on January 28, 1964.
He cut "My Heart Skips a Beat," "Ain't It Amazing Gracie," and this song that day.
Capitol saw the most potential in "My Heart Skips a Beat," so it took the A-side.
The new song went on the flip side, and Wikipedia lists Ken Nelson as producer.

The Buckaroos backed Owens, with Don Rich on guitar, fiddle, and backing vocals.
Our Buck Owens biography tells how Rich and the band built that sound.

Tom Brumley's Steel Solo

Steel guitarist Tom Brumley played the solo that defines the record.
Country Music Television staff called it "one of the finest steel guitar solos in the history of country music."
It also inspired Jerry Garcia of the Grateful Dead to learn the instrument.

The Missing Harmony

Fans who know the live version may be surprised by the original.
Owens recalled that "the hit version didn't have any harmony vocals on it at all."
Rich added his high harmony on the chorus only after the band began playing the song live.

How High Did It Chart?

"My Heart Skips a Beat" was Owens's third country No. 1.
It spent seven non-consecutive weeks on top and 26 weeks on the chart, Wikipedia notes.
Its B-side also hit No. 1, both replacing and being replaced by the A-side at the top.
Owens wrote that every time he looked at Billboard, he saw the B-side "just keep going on up the charts."

Who Covered It?

Ray Charles and the Pop Crossover

Ray Charles recorded the song in 1965 and released it as a single in March 1966.
It reached No. 19 on the Hot 100, No. 1 on the adult contemporary chart, and No. 10 on the soul chart.

Emmylou Harris Takes It Back to the Top

Emmylou Harris recorded it in June 1975 for her album Elite Hotel.
Her single reached the top of the country chart in April 1976.
In 1979 she and Owens dueted on "Play Together Again, Again," a tribute to the song they had both made famous.

Other Notable Versions

  • Ray Price, 1965, on Burning Memories
  • Kenny Rogers and Dottie West, a duet released in 1983
  • Dwight Yoakam, 2007, on Dwight Sings Buck

FAQ

Who wrote Together Again?

Buck Owens wrote it.
He took the title from a 1959 Liberty album by Tommy Duncan and the Texas Playboys.

Was it an A-side or a B-side?

It was the B-side of "My Heart Skips a Beat" on Capitol.
Both sides reached the top of the country chart.

Who played the steel guitar solo?

Tom Brumley played it.
Country Music Television staff called it one of the finest steel guitar solos in country history.

Which cover versions were hits?

Ray Charles reached number 19 on the Hot 100 and the top of the adult contemporary chart in 1966.
Emmylou Harris took it to the top of the country chart in 1976.

Is it a sad song?

The melody sounds sad, but Owens said the lyric is happy.
The singer's tears have stopped because his love has come back.

A song named for a reunion album became a Bakersfield classic and a standard for singers far beyond country.
